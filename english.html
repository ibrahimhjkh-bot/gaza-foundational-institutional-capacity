Gaza: From Designing Governance Structures to Building Institutional Capacity

Why have earlier governance arrangements struggled, and what would it take to build an administration capable of delivering services and enduring beyond a political transition?

English edition · 1 November 2026Foundational paperResearch discussion, not an endorsed governing plan

Central argument. Professional human and institutional capacity is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for effective and sustainable public administration. It must operate alongside political legitimacy, an executable legal mandate, public accountability, rights, and access to resources. Technical capability cannot, by itself, resolve the political and external constraints under which institutions operate.
This English edition presents the full foundational argument and its 12 numbered references. Introduction — From authority on paper to capacity in practice

This paper does not seek to determine who should govern Gaza or endorse Fatah, Hamas, a transitional committee, or any particular political arrangement. It asks a different question: what human and institutional foundations would any public administration need in order to perform its responsibilities? A new organisational structure, defined legal competencies and allocated funding do not automatically produce institutions capable of delivering fair and reliable public services. Without professional and operational capacity, formal authority can remain disconnected from practical implementation.

The converse is equally important. Administrative competence does not, by itself, confer political legitimacy, replace the Palestinian people's right to self-determination, or remove external restrictions on security, movement, resources and finance. Institutional capacity is therefore examined here as one essential condition among several, rather than a substitute for those wider conditions. The analyses of the Palestinian Authority under Fatah-led leadership and of Hamas-led administration in Gaza concern particular institutional practices in different contexts: they are neither comprehensive political verdicts nor claims that responsibility is identical across actors.

Past Palestinian experiences suggest that the problem has not always been a complete absence of formal institutions or qualified people. Rather, a central challenge has been the limited ability to bring them into a unified, professional administrative system governed by consistent rules, distinguishing public office from organisational loyalty, and subject to institutional and public accountability. From the establishment of the Palestinian Authority to the de facto governing system that emerged in Gaza after the 2007 division, different forms of authority developed without producing a unified and sustainable Palestinian public administration combining executive capability, representative legitimacy, and public accountability.[1] [2] [4] [5] [12]

Consequently, a proposed transitional framework should not be assessed only by its political identity, legal structure, or the body conferring its mandate. It should also be examined through more practical questions. Which human resources and organisational systems will support it? How will essential functions be identified? How will executive leaders and frontline personnel be selected? What will govern competence, promotion, performance, and accountability? How will institutions remain functional without reproducing factional administrative patterns?

Institutional capacity should be developed before, or alongside, the assumption of new executive responsibilities—but always in conjunction with legitimate and executable authority, rights protections, resources and accountability. Professional preparedness does not itself confer a right to govern, just as a formal mandate does not guarantee the capacity to deliver.

1. Lessons from the past: when formal authority did not become a unified public institution

To examine Gaza's institutional challenge, this paper considers two major experiences in contemporary Palestinian administration: the Palestinian Authority, whose leadership has been politically dominated by Fatah, and the de facto administration developed under Hamas in Gaza after 2007.

The purpose is not to rank these actors, issue comprehensive political verdicts, or disregard the different conditions they faced. Rather, it is to identify administrative and governance difficulties that developed in their respective contexts, while recognising their political, economic, and security constraints.

1.1 The Palestinian Authority: establishing institutions versus institutionalising the civil service

Since its establishment in 1994, the Palestinian Authority has helped develop ministries, public agencies, financial and legal institutions, and security organisations. Some periods also saw important public-financial-management and civil-service reforms.

However, the establishment of formal structures did not always produce sufficiently robust institutional rules to protect public employment from political or organisational influence or ensure equal opportunity in recruitment and promotion.

A World Bank report on governance and corruption in the West Bank and Gaza documented the historical use of public employment, in certain periods, to pursue political and social objectives. It identified instances in which recruitment and promotion were shaped by personal or party connections, including politically favoured appointments that did not adequately meet professional requirements. The report also noted pressures contributing to expanded government employment and the public wage bill.[1]

An academic study of the politicisation of the Palestinian civil service further examined how employment practices and employment-related benefits could reinforce political loyalty and restrict opposition, with such practices intensifying after the 2007 Palestinian split.[3]

Analytical fairness, however, requires acknowledging documented improvements. The World Bank identified considerable progress in public financial management compared with conditions in 2000. It also reported that the 2005 Civil Service Law brought greater regularity and transparency to hiring, despite continuing challenges in flexibility, oversight, and staffing. The OECD's 2024 review likewise recommended improving human-resource management, competency frameworks, continuous learning, and clarity of institutional responsibilities.[1] [2]

Therefore, the lesson from the Palestinian Authority's experience under Fatah-led administrations is not that institutions were absent or administrative knowledge did not exist. It is that building a government apparatus and reforming public finances do not, by themselves, guarantee a professional civil service with meaningful independence from centres of political influence.

The institutional test is not the number of ministries or employees, but whether recruitment, promotion, evaluation, and accountability follow stable professional rules that withstand changes in political power.

1.2 Hamas in Gaza: operational capability and the limits of institutional integration

The experience of Hamas in governing Gaza after 2007 presents a different case. It cannot be reduced to the assertion that there was no administrative capability or that institutions simply ceased operating.

Following the events of June 2007, a de facto governing system developed separately from Palestinian Authority institutions in the West Bank. The Gaza administration had to maintain public services amid the blockade, economic restrictions, repeated military operations, and the withdrawal of substantial numbers of Palestinian Authority employees from their workplaces.

A 2010 field study by the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO), examining the judiciary, education, and health sectors, found that the Hamas-led administration retained much of the inherited institutional structure while replacing parts of the workforce with personnel appointed under the new administration. It documented a parallel judicial apparatus, while some education and health services continued through practical arrangements and divisions of responsibilities involving Gaza and Ramallah. The study also examined the effect of the division on coordination between the rival governments in maintaining basic public services.[4]

A 2024 academic article by Muhammad Ahmad Seyam on Hamas government management of the security sector after the split concluded that the administration achieved a degree of security stability, citing its human and institutional preparedness and rapid response. At the same time, it examined criticisms of the overlap between the movement and formal government, including the deployment of military-wing members to reinforce security forces and the intermingling of some official and unofficial activities. This evidence concerns the security sector studied and should not automatically be generalised to all public institutions.[6]

The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) documented in 2017 that the divided civil service weakened Gaza's local institutions' ability to deliver essential services, respond to emergencies, and enforce the law. It linked these problems to staff absenteeism associated with salary crises, insufficient operating funds for Gaza ministries, duplicated functions, and unclear reporting lines.[5]

Together, these sources show operational capability in certain sectors under Hamas administration, but not the formation of a nationally unified government service integrated with the Palestinian Authority. They also raise questions concerning the relationship between the governing movement and formal institutions, including professional neutrality, accountability, and separation between a political organisation and government.

These outcomes cannot be separated from external restrictions, including Israel's blockade and restrictions on movement, trade, and financing, repeated military operations, and Palestinian political and fiscal division. Such constraints directly affected resources, planning, and continuity.[4] [5]

The lesson is not that the Gaza administration lacked all ability to operate services. It is that operational capability within a separate governing apparatus does not, on its own, establish a unified, professionally independent and accountable national public administration.

1.3 The shared institutional problem: politicisation and administrative fragmentation

Despite differences in context, several issues warrant attention when assessing any future governance framework:

  • Insufficient institutional separation between public employment and organisational affiliation, allowing political loyalties to influence recruitment, promotion, and the allocation of responsibilities.[1] [3]
  • Duplicated institutions, employment systems, and financial procedures, producing conflicting mandates and unclear lines of reporting and responsibility.[4] [5]
  • The incomplete development of a unified civil-service system capable of protecting employment rights and integrating accumulated experience under common professional criteria.[2] [5]
  • Limitations in institutional oversight and accountability, coupled with reduced coordination between public authorities and supervisory bodies.[1] [2] [12]

These difficulties must not be attributed to internal factional rivalry alone. Legal and institutional research on the Palestinian political system has also identified constitutional ambiguity and overlapping mandates as contributors to institutional deadlock. Israeli occupation and dependence on external financing remain additional constraints on institutions' ability to exercise authority.[1] [12]

The central implication is that replacing political leaders or appointing a technocratic committee is not, by itself, sufficient to correct entrenched administrative weaknesses. Reproducing the same administrative apparatus under different leaders or affiliations risks reproducing similar problems.

What is needed is a redefinition of the relationship between political authority and public office through professional rules, clearly specified duties, objective recruitment criteria, and meaningful accountability.

2. Building the foundational human-resource base

Any transitional administration needs more than traditional staffing or a sufficient headcount. This paper uses the term foundational human-resource base to describe a core of professional and executive capabilities, supported by integrated human-resource systems, capable of establishing, operating and coordinating institutions while retaining organisational knowledge and experience.

This is not merely a group of ministers, consultants, or independent experts. It comprises interconnected layers of capability. The approach draws on the OECD's review of Palestinian public administration, comparative research on merit systems and government performance, and scholarship on civil-service reform in post-conflict societies.[2] [7] [8]

Five interconnected components

1. Executive and administrative leadership

Leaders with practical experience in operating public institutions, managing people and resources, making decisions in complex settings, and translating policy priorities into measurable operational plans.

2. Sectoral and technical expertise

Qualified staff in health, education, engineering, water, energy, public finance, law, local administration, procurement, and supply chains, based on the public functions that must be restored or maintained.

3. Cross-cutting administrative capabilities

The shared functions that allow individual institutions to operate within a coherent system: human-resource management, accounting, budgeting, internal audit, procurement, contract management, legal affairs, data systems, digital transformation, monitoring, and evaluation.

4. Oversight and assurance capabilities

Staff and specialised units responsible for compliance, internal controls, risk management, verification of resource use, complaint handling, and review of decisions and procedures.

5. Operational and frontline personnel

Middle managers, technicians, engineers, supervisors, utility workers, and local service employees who turn policy and administrative decisions into everyday operations and public services.

The OECD review supports the need for shared and future-oriented skills, connecting competency development to hiring and performance management, and ensuring continuous learning opportunities for civil servants.[2]

A systematic review published in Public Administration examined 96 empirical studies published from 1991 to 2022. It found that merit-based recruitment and promotion, employment protections, neutrality, and professionalism were associated with better government performance and lower corruption. These international findings do not establish that the same outcomes would follow automatically in Gaza, but they provide an evidence base for civil-service design.[7]

Thus the challenge is not simply attracting well-qualified people. It is creating a system that assigns people to appropriate functions, allows them to perform, develop and be held accountable, and retains their knowledge within the institution.

3. What needs to be established before a transitional framework can operate?

The legal and organisational design of a proposed administration should be developed alongside a programme to establish its human and administrative capacities. Six complementary workstreams follow from public-administration reform research, the literature on post-conflict civil-service reform, and evidence on professional merit.[2] [7] [8]

1. Define public functions before allocating positions

The process should start by identifying what the transitional administration must actually do—not by distributing positions or creating additional departments. This requires functional analysis of essential services, legal mandates, decision-making authority, necessary skills, staffing levels, and operational and financial resources.

The resulting organisational structure should enable those functions, rather than become an end in itself. The OECD review emphasises clear leadership and executive responsibilities, identifiable implementation and monitoring bodies, and reforms tied to defined results and assessable responsibilities.[2]

2. Undertake a unified assessment of existing personnel and capabilities

A professional personnel inventory should cover current and former employees in government institutions, municipalities, and public utilities, documenting qualifications, actual experience, specialisations, skills, and roles individuals may be qualified to perform.

It must not become a mechanism for collective exclusion based on political affiliation or the circumstances of original appointment. Individual assessment should consider qualifications, experience, and employment records while protecting legal rights, appeals, and independent review.

The need for this assessment is heightened by the duplicated staff records, salary systems, and administrative authorities documented in studies of the Palestinian institutional division.[4] [5]

3. Establish a professionally independent recruitment and selection mechanism

A body with meaningful independence from political patronage should oversee recruitment for executive and professional positions against published criteria, including education, experience, technical competence, management capability, and professional integrity.

This requires public job descriptions, transparent eligibility requirements, competitive selection procedures, documented decisions, and a mechanism for objections and review. Professional independence does not mean the absence of political, legal, or public oversight. It means protecting recruitment from factional allocation while holding the administration to rules of law, transparency, and accountability. The OECD review and the systematic merit-based governance literature support this direction.[2] [7]

4. Continuously develop leadership and executive capabilities

Recruitment based on qualifications and previous experience is not sufficient. Staff must also be prepared to work within new administrative arrangements. Training should cover public leadership, crisis response, planning, project management, budgeting, public procurement, contract administration, digital systems, internal controls, monitoring, and evaluation.

Middle managers and technical personnel deserve particular attention because they link policy decisions with service delivery. OECD recommends developing shared and future-oriented capabilities and connecting them to recruitment, performance management, and continuous learning. Scholarship on civil-service reform in post-conflict societies also underscores the importance of building lasting public institutions rather than relying exclusively on temporary service-delivery arrangements.[2] [8]

5. Integrate administrative, financial, and operational systems

A unified public administration cannot operate effectively if its constituent units apply separate rules for recruitment, payroll, purchasing, data, and reporting. A coherent system therefore requires compatible human-resource management, verified personnel records, auditable payroll, common accounting and procurement standards, contract-management rules, and clear lines of authority and reporting.

A transitional arrangement should not simply create another layer of parallel agencies or replicate existing functions. It should progressively resolve duplication and clarify financial and administrative authority. Palestinian institutional experience illustrates the risks of duplicated roles and unclear reporting. Post-conflict reform research warns that donor-supported parallel systems may help deliver services in the short term yet undermine long-term institutional development if they never become integrated into public administration.[5] [8]

6. Establish rules for neutrality and accountability

Recruitment standards alone cannot prevent the recurrence of earlier weaknesses. They should be accompanied by a civil-service code of conduct, conflict-of-interest provisions, transparent performance standards, and relatively independent audit, oversight, and complaint mechanisms.

Public employees should be protected against discrimination based on political or organisational affiliation while remaining accountable for professional conduct and performance. Leaders and staff should be evaluated against service quality, resource efficiency, legal compliance, and actual results—not merely procedural compliance or numerical indicators disconnected from public impact. International evidence links merit, neutrality and professionalism with better government performance and lower corruption, without establishing automatic causality in Gaza.[7]

4. Conditions that turn qualified people into effective institutions

A professionally qualified workforce does not, by itself, resolve the political, security and fiscal context. An administration may employ highly capable people yet remain unable to exercise its responsibilities without real authority, access to workplaces, freedom to move personnel and resources, and reliable operating funds.

Capacity building should therefore be accompanied by five enabling conditions:

First: political commitment to civil-service neutrality

Employment, promotion, and responsibility allocation should not be tools of organisational patronage. Workers should be protected against discrimination based on affiliation. Research on Palestinian civil-service politicisation supports addressing this problem as part of administrative reform.[3]

Second: a clearly defined relationship with the Palestinian Authority

Administrative, financial, and legal responsibilities, mechanisms for transferring functions, and the relationship between transitional arrangements and existing government institutions must be clarified within an assessable process. Research on the Palestinian constitutional crisis also demonstrates the importance of resolving overlapping competencies among constitutional and political institutions.[12]

Third: professional autonomy with accountability

Routine administrative and technical decisions should be protected against undue day-to-day political interference, without making administrative independence an exemption from legal, civic, or institutional oversight.

Fourth: an executive mandate that can actually be exercised

Practical arrangements must enable workers to access service areas, public facilities, and resources within their responsibility, under conditions that permit safe service delivery and legal enforcement. A skilled public workforce without executable authority leaves the gap between formal mandate and operational reality unresolved.

Fifth: sustainable operating finance

International financing should not be limited to reconstruction projects and capital investment. Staffing, training, maintenance, operations, and the administrative and control systems necessary for continued service delivery must also be accounted for.

Nor should it be assumed that restoring public services will automatically grant political legitimacy to a new administration. Claire McLoughlin's research in Governance finds that the relationship between services and state legitimacy in fragile or conflict-affected settings is not assured or linear. It is shaped by people's expectations, perceptions of fairness and impartiality, and their relationship with the service provider.[11]

Service efficiency matters, but cannot substitute for equity, transparency, representation, or accountability.

5. Municipalities and existing institutions: operational platforms, not substitutes for national governance

Municipalities and established public utilities can play an important part in restoring services because their responsibilities connect directly to water, waste management, roads, environmental health, local planning, and other daily needs.

However, these local bodies should not automatically be assumed to possess the staffing, organisational systems, or finances needed to take on broader responsibilities. Research on Palestinian local government has identified overlapping mandates, weak institutional integration, fragmented legal and administrative structures, and the impact of occupation and political division.[9] [10]

Municipalities should therefore be considered possible local operational platforms within a coordinated national public-administration framework, with strengthened technical, administrative and financial capacity, clearer relations with central government, and safeguards against turning decentralisation into permanent institutional fragmentation.

This requires consistent service standards, transparent resource allocation, coherent professional management of staff, and arrangements for coordination and oversight connecting local delivery organisations with the broader public system.

6. How should progress toward institutional capacity be measured?

To avoid reducing human-capacity development to training workshops and formal recruitment exercises, institutional progress should be assessed with verifiable measures.

AreaProposed indicator
Coverage of critical functionsShare of essential posts filled by personnel meeting documented job requirements.
Merit-based recruitmentShare of appointments made through documented competitive procedures and published criteria.
Professional readinessExtent to which staff meet required competencies and complete role-relevant training.
Operational continuityRegularity of salaries, staff stability, and continuity of essential services.
Administrative integrationUnified staffing and payroll registers, clarity of reporting lines, and reduction in duplicated functions.
Integrity and accountabilityAudit findings, responsiveness to complaints, compliance with conflict-of-interest rules, and regular public reporting.
Equity of service accessFairness of service availability across communities and areas, without political or organisational discrimination.

These measures are proposed by this paper; they are not empirical indicators already measured in Gaza. Their design reflects principles of performance management, transparency, and accountability discussed in the OECD review and comparative research on civil-service merit and government performance.[2] [7]

Conclusion — A governing mandate requires an institution capable of carrying it out

Past Palestinian experiences indicate that formal institutions and the ability to keep some services running are not sufficient, by themselves, to establish a coherent, durable public-governance system.

The Palestinian Authority's experience illustrates that institution building and public-financial reforms do not necessarily ensure a professionally independent and accountable civil service. The experience of Hamas-led administration in Gaza, meanwhile, suggests that operational capability in particular sectors does not establish a unified national system in which executive functions, political legitimacy, and accountability are integrated.[1] [3] [4] [5]

A new governance framework should therefore not be judged merely by the number of institutions it creates, the individuals appointed to lead them, or the financial pledges associated with it. Its added value depends on whether it creates a public-service apparatus with the necessary skills, systems, resources and lines of accountability to turn legal authority into sustainable institutional performance.

The strategic focus needs to move from drawing an organisational chart to developing its human foundation; from allocating positions to identifying functions and competencies; from replacing leaders to reforming recruitment, evaluation and accountability; and from creating new bodies to rebuilding institutions' ability to function.

An administration is not technocratic simply because its members are non-partisan; it is not institutional simply because it has an organisational chart; and it does not become capable of governing merely by receiving a legal mandate.

These qualities require a professional public-service system operating under stable rules, meaningful institutional and civic oversight, and published standards for service quality and people's rights, regardless of who occupies office.

Yet this vision does not place technical administration above politics, or suggest that management reform can substitute for legitimate representation, self-determination, or action on the restrictions imposed on movement and resources. The paper identifies what must be built within public institutions while recognising the wider political, legal and external conditions required for them to operate. Examining past institutional weaknesses is not an endorsement of a competing political actor or an assertion of equal responsibility for the broader situation.

One question should therefore precede the adoption of any new transitional framework:

Are we designing a temporary administration for Gaza, or building a public institution that can endure beyond the transition and changes in leadership and political authority?

The genuine test of any proposed model is not only whether it can begin operating, but whether it can produce institutions that continue to function when the people leading them change.
